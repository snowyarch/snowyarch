# DebtWatch

**When does expensive credit become unavailable credit?**

Research and monitoring · Historical snapshot: **2 October 2026** · Public edition: **3 October 2026**

I follow how macro pressure reaches credit markets and individual companies. The interesting question is not whether financing has become uncomfortable. It is whether the evidence shows a change in what borrowers can actually obtain, on what terms, and with what consequences.

DebtWatch is a research workflow, not a crash predictor. “Not yet” is a useful result when the evidence does not support the stronger claim.

## The transmission question

The conceptual path runs from macro conditions through interest rates and credit pricing to refinancing and company cash flows. It is not an automatic sequence. Timing, debt maturity, fixed versus floating rates, collateral, lender appetite and operating performance can change how pressure travels.

| Layer | Question | What the observation cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Macro conditions | What could change the cost or supply of financing? | That every borrower will experience the same constraint |
| Credit pricing | Are lenders demanding more compensation for risk? | That financing is unavailable |
| Refinancing | Can borrowers replace maturing debt, and on what terms? | That one successful or failed transaction describes the market |
| Company consequences | How do interest expense, maturities and contractual terms affect flexibility? | That a difficult capital structure necessarily produces default |

The distinction I keep returning to is between **price, terms and access**. A borrower may refinance at a higher cost. Another may obtain funds only after providing additional security or accepting tighter restrictions. A third may fail to obtain the financing it needs. These are different observations, and the public argument should keep them separate.

## What this snapshot supports

The reviewed material supported a narrower interpretation than a general credit-access breakdown: pressure was more evident in pricing, with selective deterioration in contractual terms, while broad failure of access had not been established by the available sample.

That conclusion describes the reviewed evidence at its historical cutoff. It is not a claim about conditions today. The 2 October review assessed earlier evidence, including a targeted borrower review cut off on 1 October; it was not an independent, comprehensive collection of every transaction on 2 October.

Successful refinancings matter. They are counterevidence to an indiscriminate claim that the market has closed. They do not establish that all borrowers are healthy, that terms are benign, or that future refinancing will be easy. Equally, a failed transaction deserves attention without becoming proof of an economy-wide failure.

## Why the denominator matters

The borrower panel was selected after identifying cases of interest. It was not a representative sample of all eligible borrowers. Counting stressed cases in that panel cannot produce a valid estimate of the proportion of the market losing access to credit.

To make that stronger claim, the research would need a defined population, consistent eligibility rules, an observation window, and comparable treatment of successful transactions and failures. A larger list of alarming examples does not, by itself, repair a missing denominator.

The monitoring discipline is to ask what changed, what stayed functional, which alternative explanation still fits, and what evidence would distinguish the alternatives. A development that looks important may change the question without settling it.

## What would change the assessment?

More persuasive evidence would connect worsening terms or failed refinancing to a clearly defined borrower population and show that the pattern persists beyond isolated cases. Evidence of continued access, improved terms, or operating resilience would also change the assessment. Both directions belong in the record.

“Not yet” therefore means **the available evidence has not crossed the threshold for the stronger conclusion**. It does not mean no risk exists, or that a future breakdown is inevitable.
